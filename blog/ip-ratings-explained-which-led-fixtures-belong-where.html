IP Ratings Explained — Which LED Fixtures Belong Where in a Home or Commercial Build

IP65, IP67, IP68 explained in plain terms — which LED fixtures need which IP rating for bathrooms, kitchens, outdoor and indoor use. Guide from Solid State Lights India.

“IP65 rated” gets printed on lighting spec sheets constantly, and most buyers nod along without knowing what it actually means — until a fixture fails in a bathroom six months in because someone specified an indoor-only downlight in a wet zone. IP ratings aren't marketing fluff; they're a real, testable standard for what a fixture can survive. This guide decodes the numbers and tells you exactly which rating belongs in which room.

From the team at Solid State Lights — every product page on our site lists the correct IP rating; this explains what those numbers mean in practice.

What IP Actually Stands For

IP stands for Ingress Protection — an international standard (IEC 60529) rating how well an enclosure resists solid objects (dust, fingers, tools) and liquids (splashes, jets, submersion). It's written as IP followed by two digits: the first digit rates solid-particle protection (0–6), the second rates liquid protection (0–9).

So IP65 means: 6 (fully dust-tight) + 5 (protected against water jets from any direction). IP68 means: 6 (dust-tight) + 8 (protected against continuous submersion beyond 1 metre depth, tested per manufacturer spec).

The First Digit — Solid Particle Protection

  • IP0X: No protection
  • IP4X: Protected against tools and wires thicker than 1mm
  • IP5X: Dust-protected — limited ingress allowed, doesn't affect operation
  • IP6X: Fully dust-tight — no ingress at all. This is what almost every quality indoor and outdoor LED fixture should carry.

The Second Digit — Liquid Protection

  • IPX0: No protection
  • IPX2: Protected against dripping water at an angle — basic indoor fixtures
  • IPX4: Protected against splashing water from any direction — suitable for kitchens, semi-open areas
  • IPX5: Protected against low-pressure water jets — bathrooms, exterior walls under eaves
  • IPX6: Protected against powerful water jets — fully exposed outdoor walls, façades
  • IPX7: Protected against temporary immersion up to 1 metre for 30 minutes — flood-prone outdoor areas
  • IPX8: Protected against continuous submersion beyond 1 metre — underwater and permanently wet applications

Which Rating for Which Room — A Practical Table

  • Living rooms, bedrooms, offices, corridors: IP20 is fine — standard indoor COB downlights and LED panels
  • Kitchens (general area): IP44 minimum — splash protection near the sink and hob zone
  • Bathrooms outside the shower zone: IP44–IP65 depending on proximity to water
  • Bathrooms directly above a shower or bath: IP65 minimum — direct water jet exposure
  • Covered outdoor areas (verandas, porches): IP54–IP65
  • Fully exposed outdoor walls, façade lighting: IP65–IP66
  • Ground-level pathway and landscape lighting: IP65–IP67 (rain pooling risk)
  • Fountains, ponds, submerged pool lighting: IP68 only — nothing less is safe for continuous submersion

Common Mistakes We See in Project Specs

  • Using IP20 indoor downlights above a shower. This is a genuine electrical safety hazard, not just a durability issue — Indian electrical codes require IP65+ in direct wet zones.
  • Assuming “IP65” on the box means it's fine anywhere outdoors. IP65 handles jets, not submersion. A pathway light in a monsoon-prone low-lying area needs IP67 at minimum — standing water is a different failure mode than splashing.
  • Over-specifying IP68 for dry indoor use. IP68 fixtures cost more and aren't necessary for a living room ceiling — match the rating to the actual exposure, don't just buy the highest number available.
  • Ignoring the driver's IP rating separately. The fixture body might be IP65 but if the driver/transformer sits exposed in a non-rated housing, the overall installation is only as protected as its weakest link.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does IP65 mean in simple terms?

IP65 means the fixture is completely dust-tight (the '6') and protected against low-pressure water jets from any direction (the '5'). It's the standard rating for bathrooms and semi-exposed outdoor areas, but not for continuous submersion.

Can I use an IP20 downlight in a kitchen?

Only in areas away from the sink and hob splash zone. Directly above or near water sources, use IP44 minimum for safety and longevity.

What's the difference between IP65 and IP68?

IP65 handles water jets and splashing but not submersion. IP68 is rated for continuous submersion beyond 1 metre — required for pool, fountain and permanently underwater applications.

Is a higher IP rating always better?

Not necessarily for cost and design reasons — a living room ceiling doesn't need IP68 protection. Match the rating to the actual exposure the fixture will face; over-specifying adds cost without benefit.

Does the IP rating apply to the whole fixture including the driver?

The rating should apply to the complete installed system. A well-rated fixture body with an exposed, non-rated driver is only as protected as its weakest component — check both when specifying.
